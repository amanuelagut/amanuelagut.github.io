Grain Wholesale Prices: Ibagué vs. Bogotá During the Pandemic

Angie Manuela Gutierrez-Oviedo · Fundación Universitaria del Área Andina · 2020
SIPSA-DANE Corabastos · Bogotá Plaza de la 21 · Ibagué Price Spread Analysis Market Integration Regional Comparison COVID-19
Abstract
Resumen
This brief compares wholesale grain price trajectories between Ibagué (Plaza de la 21, a regional production hub in Tolima) and Bogotá (Corabastos, the national distribution center) during the COVID-19 pandemic. Using SIPSA-DANE weekly wholesale price data aggregated to monthly averages, the analysis documents regional price divergence as a measure of market integration under supply chain stress. The Bogotá-Ibagué price spread for fríjol widened from 8.2% to 14.7% during peak lockdown (April–June 2020), indicating temporary market segmentation. Rice showed minimal divergence (spread remained stable at 6–8%), reflecting its more formalized supply chain. The analysis is grounded in direct operational knowledge of both markets from the author's experience in grain distribution in Ibagué.
Keywords: market integration, regional price spread, wholesale markets, supply chain disruption, COVID-19, agricultural logistics
1.
Research Question
Pregunta de Investigación

How did wholesale grain prices diverge between Ibagué and Bogotá during COVID-19, and what does this reveal about regional market integration under supply chain disruption?

Ibagué is a net grain producer (rice, sorghum) located 200 km west of Bogotá, while Bogotá is a net consumer with 8 million inhabitants. Under normal conditions, price differences between the two reflect transport costs (~COP 150–200/kg) and intermediary margins. During COVID-19, mobility restrictions disrupted transport networks — testing whether the price gap widened (market segmentation) or remained stable (robust integration).

Three products are tracked in both markets: fríjol bolo rojo (highest volatility), arroz paddy verde (rice, most stable), and maíz amarillo (corn, import-sensitive). The March–December 2020 window captures the full lockdown (March 20 declaration) through partial reopening (September onwards).

3.
Methodology
Metodología

The analysis uses pivot tables and price spread computation to systematically compare two markets across multiple products.

Step 1 — Pivot table construction. Monthly average prices organized by product × city × month using Excel pivot tables. This produces a structured comparison matrix (3 products × 2 cities × 10 months) that makes city-level divergence immediately readable.

Step 2 — Price spread computation. For each product-month pair, the percentage price difference between Bogotá and Ibagué is computed: Spread% = ((P_Bogotá − P_Ibagué) / P_Ibagué) × 100. A positive spread indicates Bogotá is more expensive (the expected direction given transport costs).

Step 3 — Spread volatility analysis. Standard deviation and CV of the monthly spread series quantify how much the inter-market relationship fluctuated during the pandemic. Higher spread volatility indicates market integration breakdown.

Step 4 — Base-100 dual index. Both city series are indexed to March 2020 = 100 per product, creating overlapping time series that isolate divergence patterns from absolute price levels.

4.
Key Finding
Hallazgo Principal

The regional comparison reveals that market integration weakened measurably during peak lockdown months, with product-specific patterns reflecting supply chain characteristics.

Key Finding
Hallazgo Principal
Fríjol: The Bogotá-Ibagué price spread widened from a baseline of 8.2% (March 2020) to a peak of 14.7% (May 2020), then narrowed to 9.4% by October 2020 as mobility restrictions eased. Bogotá mean price: COP 6,847/kg; Ibagué mean: COP 6,012/kg; spread volatility (CV): 24.3%.

Arroz (Rice): The spread remained stable at 6–8% throughout the period, with minimal pandemic-related disruption. Bogotá mean: COP 2,156/kg; Ibagué mean: COP 2,014/kg; spread volatility (CV): 8.1%. Rice's formalized supply chain (larger-scale producers, established logistics) proved more resilient.

Maíz (Corn): The spread showed moderate widening from 9.1% (March) to 12.8% (April), then stabilized at 10–11%. Bogotá mean: COP 1,234/kg; Ibagué mean: COP 1,112/kg. Corn's partial import dependency dampened regional divergence as international prices transmitted uniformly.

Key insight: Market integration is product-specific. Perishable or logistics-sensitive products (fríjol) showed larger disruption; formalized chains (rice) maintained integration even under stress.

5.
What I Learned
Lo que Aprendí

This project introduced pivot table analysis as a systematic tool for cross-dimensional comparison — organizing data by product × city × time in a way that makes patterns visible without complex computation. This becomes the conceptual foundation for SQL GROUP BY queries in 2021.

The concept of market integration — the idea that spatially separated markets should converge in price under efficient arbitrage — is foundational in agricultural economics. Measuring the price spread empirically connected my operational experience (knowing the Ibagué-Bogotá transport routes and costs) to formal economic theory.

Building matched comparisons (same product, same time, different location) taught the importance of controlled comparison design — a principle that scales to the panel regression frameworks used in 2025.

6.
Limitation
Limitación
Data Limitation
Datos Limitación
The Ibagué market (Plaza de la 21) has less consistent SIPSA reporting than Corabastos, leading to occasional missing weekly observations that required interpolation. Transport cost data — which would directly explain the price spread — is not available at the frequency needed. The 2022 RB1 (Oil Price Shocks and Transport Costs) partially addresses this by using diesel prices as a transport cost proxy. A formal cointegration test between the two market series would provide statistical confirmation of market integration — this is implemented in the 2024 Python workflow.
7.
Policy Implication
Implicaciones de Politica

The product-specific market integration patterns observed during COVID-19 suggest that emergency logistics planning should prioritize supply chains for products with informal distribution networks (like fríjol) over those with formalized channels (like rice). The 79% widening of the Bogotá-Ibagué fríjol spread during lockdown indicates that mobility restrictions disproportionately affected smaller-scale distributors.

For regional development policy, the findings support investment in logistics infrastructure connecting secondary markets (Ibagué) to primary distribution centers (Bogotá), as price transmission efficiency varies significantly by product type and supply chain formalization level.
